Introduction

The Salem Witch Trials are often remembered as a dramatic hunt for witches, superstition and mass hysteria. But the story is far bigger than that. This is a journey through multiple perspectives, historical evidence and theories, to see how gender, social power and religion shaped the accusations, and to question how history is understood and remembered.

The Beginning

01 · Salem in 1690's

1692 Salem

Salem, Massachusetts, was a divided community. Religion was central to everyday life for the Puritans: people strongly believed in God, Satan, sin and supernatural forces, within a strict social hierarchy. Yet beneath the surface, tensions were already building over land, wealth and authority.

02 · What happened?

January 1692

Something changed. Two young girls, Betty Parris, 9, and Abigail Williams, 11, began having unexplained fits and unusual behaviour. They screamed, threw things, twisted their bodies and said they were being tormented. With no ordinary explanation, a local doctor declared them under the influence of evil forces, and a wave of fear and suspicion began to spread.

04 · Confessions & hysteria

A Forced Confession

Tituba eventually confessed to practising witchcraft and claimed other witches were working with the Devil. She had never practised witchcraft or harmed anyone. Her owner, Samuel Parris, father of Betty and a Puritan minister, forced her to confess through severe physical abuse. She feared she would be hanged if she denied it, and figured that confessing would stop the torture and keep her alive in jail.

Her confession set off more fear. The accusations spread.

Factors: Why Did It Happen?
i

05 · Factor 1 of 5

Religion & Fear

There is no single explanation. Historians point to religious beliefs, community conflict, fear, economic instability and personal disputes. The Puritan Church reinforced the idea that the Devil was constantly working to corrupt society, which created fear within the community.

ii

06 · Factor 2 of 5

The Role of the Church

Any unauthorised ritual other than official scripture was viewed by the Puritans as making a pact with the Devil. The church held enormous power over what counted as acceptable belief, and over who was seen as a threat.

iii

07 · Factor 3 of 5

Women & Gender

Women were targeted because of deeply rooted beliefs that they were more vulnerable to temptation and more likely to be influenced by the devil. Women with healing knowledge were usually tolerated, but a sudden illness or failed cure made people wonder if unseen forces were involved.

iv

08 · Factor 4 of 5

Poverty & Social Class

The first people accused were lower-class women: poor, widowed, unmarried or socially marginalised. They had less power and protection within society, which made them easier targets.

v

09 · Factor 5 of 5

Community Conflict

Accusations rose from disputes over land, money, inheritance and neighbours, especially between traditional farmers and wealthier merchants. Once a person was charged, their property could be seized. Accusations became a weapon, and the trials soon broke gender boundaries as men were accused and executed too.

The Trials

10 · Accusations & executions

Hysteria Spreads

Accusations moved from the three women to neighbours, family and respected members of the community. Governor William Phips established the Court of Oyer and Terminer to accept spectral evidence: the claim that an accused person's spirit had appeared to someone. The first execution took place on June 10, 1692: Bridget Bishop.

Although they are called the 'Salem' Witch Trials, accusations spread into surrounding communities and across Europe, including Germany, Poland, France and England.

12 · Common misconceptions

"It was only about witchcraft."

There is no single explanation. Land disputes, inheritance, social class and fear all played a part, and accusations became an effective weapon against rivals.

13 · Common misconceptions

"It only happened in Salem."

Accusations spread into surrounding communities and across Europe, where an estimated 40,000 to 60,000 people were executed.

14 · Common misconceptions

"Tituba really practised witchcraft."

She never did. Her confession was forced through severe physical abuse by her owner, because she feared being hanged.

Chapter 4 of 4
The End

15 · The trials come to an end

Emerging

The trials took place between 1692 and 1693 and eventually ended, leaving Salem to deal with the consequences. Some participants admitted their mistakes, and in 1697 the Massachusetts General Court called for a day of fasting and repentance.

16 · The aftermath & legacy

Compensation was later provided to some families. The trials remain a reminder of how fear, power and prejudice can shape what a community believes, and how history is remembered.
